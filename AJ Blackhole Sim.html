The equations behind the picture

Units are chosen so the starting event horizon has radius 1 (written \(r_s = 1\)). Read them in order: each step builds on the one before.

1

How big is the black hole?

The Schwarzschild radius is the size of the event horizon. It grows in step with the mass: TON 618's is about 800 times the Earth–Sun distance.

\[ r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2} \]
2

How space-time is curved

The Schwarzschild metric measures distances and times around a non-spinning mass. Close to \(r_s\) the factor \(1 - r_s/r\) shrinks towards zero: clocks slow and space stretches.

\[ ds^2 = -\Big(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\Big)c^2\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1-\dfrac{r_s}{r}} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right) \]
3

The path of a light ray

Solving the metric for light gives one orbit equation in \(u = 1/r\). The right-hand side is all of Einstein's light bending. The simulator steps its 3D form a few hundred times per pixel, every frame.

\[ \frac{d^2u}{d\phi^2} + u = \frac{3}{2}\, r_s\, u^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d^2\mathbf{x}}{d\lambda^2} = -\frac{3}{2}\, r_s\, h^2\, \frac{\mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}|^5} \]
4

The shadow and the disk

Light can circle the hole at \(1.5\,r_s\), so the shadow looks about 2.6 times bigger than the horizon. Gas orbits down to \(3\,r_s\), hotter further in, and the side moving towards you looks brighter.

\[ b_c = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\, r_s, \qquad r_{\text{in}} = 3\, r_s, \qquad T \propto r^{-3/4}, \qquad I_{\text{seen}} \propto g^3 \]
5

How planets, stars and your spaceship move

Everything with mass feels Newton's pull plus an extra Einstein term. No circular orbit is stable inside \(3\,r_s\): there, no amount of sideways speed keeps you up, which is why your ship needs full thrust to escape.

\[ \frac{d^2\mathbf{x}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{r_s}{2}\,\frac{\mathbf{x}}{r^3} \;-\; \frac{3}{2}\, r_s\, h^2\,\frac{\mathbf{x}}{r^5}, \qquad v_{\text{circ}}^2 = \frac{r_s/2}{\,r - \tfrac{3}{2}r_s\,} \]
6

Tides, quasars and mergers

Objects break up inside the tidal radius. Infalling matter turns about 6% of its mass into light, enough to make a quasar. Two black holes radiate gravitational waves, spiral together and lose about 5% of their mass when they merge.

\[ r_t \approx R_\star\left(\frac{M_{\bullet}}{M_\star}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad L = \eta\,\dot{M}\,c^2,\ \ \eta \approx 0.057, \qquad M_{\text{final}} \approx 0.95\,(m_1+m_2) \]
7

Falling in

Dropped from far away, you fall at the local escape speed. Tides scale as \(r_s/r^3\), so at the horizon they go as \(1/r_s^2\): the bigger the black hole, the gentler its horizon.

\[ \frac{dr}{d\tau} = -c\sqrt{\frac{r_s}{r}}, \qquad \Delta a_{\text{tide}} = \frac{c^2 r_s\,L}{r^3} \]
8

How big it looks from Earth

The shadow's apparent size is its width divided by its distance. Even our nearest supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A*, spans about 50 microarcseconds: like a doughnut on the Moon. It took a telescope network as wide as Earth to see it.

\[ \theta_{\text{shadow}} \approx \frac{2 b_c}{D} = \frac{3\sqrt{3}\, r_s}{D} \]
